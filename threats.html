Apps that invade your phone

A detailed breakdown of the specific app types used to monitor activity, access files, and intercept traffic — and exactly how to detect and remove each one.

Category 01

Phone Activity Monitoring Apps

Apps that silently record everything you do — who you call, what you type, where you go, and what you say.

📊
Activity & Communication Surveillance
Targets: calls, SMS, keystrokes, location, screen content
CRITICAL
Call & SMS Interceptors
Access: CALL_LOG · READ_SMS · RECORD_AUDIO
▶

These apps silently log all incoming and outgoing calls (duration, number, recording), read and copy every SMS and MMS message, and upload everything to a remote server. They disguise themselves as "backup", "sync", or "system" utilities.

How they work
  • Register as a CallLog ContentObserver — gets every call event silently
  • Use Android's READ_SMS permission to mirror all incoming messages to a remote number or server
  • On iOS, exploits iCloud backup access or MDM profiles to extract call history
  • Some use RECORD_AUDIO to capture call audio via the phone's microphone directly
Warning signs on your phone
  • Unknown contacts receiving your messages (SMS forwarding)
  • Data usage spike with no new apps or streaming
  • Slight delay before calls connect (interception overhead)
  • Battery draining during idle periods
  • Microphone indicator light appearing unprompted
How to detect & remove
  • Settings → Privacy → Permission Manager → Microphone / Phone — revoke anything suspicious
  • Check SMS forwarding: Message app settings → no unfamiliar numbers should be listed
  • Android: Settings → Apps → show system apps — look for duplicates of system names
  • iOS: Settings → General → VPN & Device Management — remove any unknown MDM profiles
  • Factory reset if the app has Device Administrator privileges you can't revoke
Known commercial spyware (documented)
  • mSpy — marketed as "parental control", used for partner surveillance
  • FlexiSPY — explicit call interception capability, sold openly
  • Hoverwatch — call log + SMS mirror + location tracker
  • Pegasus (NSO Group) — state-level, zero-click call interception via WhatsApp/iMessage exploit

Keyloggers & Screen Recorders
Access: ACCESSIBILITY · WRITE_SETTINGS · MEDIA_PROJECTION
▶

Keyloggers record every keystroke you make — passwords, messages, searches. Screen recorders take continuous screenshots or video of your display. Both abuse Android's Accessibility Service, which was designed for disability access but gives near-total control of the screen and inputs.

How they work
  • Register an AccessibilityService that receives every TYPE, CLICK, and VIEW event
  • Reconstruct typed text character by character, including passwords
  • Use MediaProjection API (Android 5+) to capture the screen at intervals
  • Overlay attacks: draw invisible windows over legitimate apps to capture input before it reaches the real app
Warning signs
  • Accessibility settings show an unfamiliar service enabled
  • Significant CPU usage when you're actively typing
  • Screen sometimes flickers (screenshot capture artifact)
  • App appears in overlay permission list (draw over other apps)
  • Device feels sluggish during input-heavy tasks
How to detect & remove
  • Settings → Accessibility → Installed Services — only keep apps you recognise
  • Settings → Apps → (three dots) → Special App Access → Display over other apps — review all
  • Google Play Protect scan: Play Store → Profile icon → Play Protect → Scan
  • On iOS: keyloggers require physical access + jailbreak, or a custom keyboard app granted Full Access — Settings → General → Keyboard → Keyboards → check for unknown keyboards
Known examples
  • TouchLogger — exploits motion sensors to infer keystrokes without permission
  • Android/SpyAgent — screen-grabbing RAT disguised as utility apps
  • iKeyMonitor — iOS/Android keylogger sold as parental tool
  • TheTruthSpy — keylogger + GPS + media access sold openly until 2022

Silent Location & GPS Trackers
Access: ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION · BACKGROUND_LOCATION
▶

Apps that continuously track your precise GPS location in the background and report it to a remote server — often without ever appearing in your recent apps list. They use low-power geofencing to stay hidden while still triangulating your position every few minutes.

How they work
  • Request ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION to track even when app is closed
  • Use WorkManager or AlarmManager to schedule silent location uploads every 2–5 minutes
  • Use cell tower triangulation as fallback when GPS is off (no permission required for coarse location via network)
  • Register BOOT_COMPLETED to restart automatically after phone reboot
Warning signs
  • Location icon in notification bar when you're not using maps
  • Battery drain spike when you leave the house (location polling increases)
  • Unknown app listed under Settings → Location → App permissions → All the time
  • Small regular data transfers to unfamiliar IP ranges
How to detect & remove
  • Settings → Location → App permissions → "Allow all the time" — any unfamiliar app here is a red flag
  • iOS: Settings → Privacy → Location Services — look for "Always" permissions on unrecognised apps
  • Turn off location for apps that have no legitimate need for it
  • Use a network monitor app (NetGuard, Little Snitch on iOS) to catch silent uploads
Known examples
  • Life360 — legitimate family app, also sold location data to data brokers until 2021
  • Cerberus — anti-theft app repurposed as stalkerware
  • GeoZilla — background tracking, data retention concerns
  • Stalkerware bundles (Android) — often disguised as "Find My Phone" clones
ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION RECEIVE_BOOT_COMPLETED FOREGROUND_SERVICE
📂
File & Data Access Trojans
Targets: photos, documents, contacts, app data, credentials
HIGH
Storage Scrapers (Photos, Docs, Credentials)
Access: READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE · MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE
▶

These apps request broad storage access — often framed as a "cleaner" or "photo editor" — and silently copy your photos, PDFs, documents, and downloaded files to a remote server. On Android 11+ they require MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE which is a rare permission that should trigger immediate suspicion in any non-file-manager app.

How they work
  • Walk the entire DCIM, Downloads, and Documents directories recursively
  • Filter for high-value files: .pdf, .doc, .xls, .jpg, .png, .key, .p12 (certificate files)
  • Compress and encrypt files before uploading to avoid detection
  • Abuse MediaStore API to access photos without explicit storage permission on some Android versions
  • On iOS: exploit photo library access to enumerate and exfiltrate without triggering obvious prompts
Warning signs
  • Large unexplained data upload (check Settings → Mobile Data → usage per app)
  • "Cleaner" or "utility" app requesting photo / file access
  • App behaviour doesn't match its stated purpose
  • High disk I/O when phone is idle (copying files to temp folder)
How to detect & remove
  • Settings → Apps → Special App Access → All files access — only file managers need this
  • Settings → Privacy → Photos — any app with Full Access that isn't a photo editor is suspicious
  • Check data usage: Settings → Network → Data Usage — sort by consumption, look for anomalies
  • Use iOS "App Privacy Report" (Settings → Privacy → App Privacy Report) to see which apps accessed your files
Known examples
  • SparkCat (2024) — disguised as AI tools, used OCR to steal crypto seed phrases from photos
  • Android/FakeApp — utility app wrappers that silently exfiltrate document storage
  • Multiple "cleaner" apps removed from Play Store 2022–2024 for mass file collection

Contact & Calendar Harvesters
Access: READ_CONTACTS · READ_CALENDAR · GET_ACCOUNTS
▶

Harvest your full contact list, calendar events (meeting names, locations, attendees), and linked account list. Sold to data brokers or used to launch highly personalised spear-phishing attacks against you and everyone in your contacts.

How they work
  • Query the ContactsContract ContentProvider — gets names, numbers, emails, addresses of every contact
  • Read calendar entries including private appointment notes and video call links
  • GET_ACCOUNTS lists every account linked to the device (Google, bank apps, social media)
  • Upload silently to ad-tech or surveillance platforms via HTTPS to avoid detection
How to detect & remove
  • Settings → Privacy → Contacts — review every app with access. Deny anything that doesn't obviously need it
  • iOS App Privacy Report shows a timeline of which apps accessed contacts and when
  • Revoke GET_ACCOUNTS via adb or in developer options on Android if available
  • Be extremely suspicious of flashlight, wallpaper, or game apps requesting contacts

Category 02

Multi-Layer Bluetooth Attacks

Bluetooth attacks operate invisibly within 10–100m. Modern attacks use relay chains to extend range far beyond what you'd expect.

🔵
Bluetooth Attack Vectors
Range: 10–100m direct · Unlimited with relay chains
HIGH
BlueSnarfing — Silent Data Extraction
Layer: OBEX protocol · Target: contacts, calendar, files
▶

BlueSnarfing exploits the OBEX Object Push protocol in Bluetooth to pull files from your device without pairing or confirmation. It specifically targets contacts (vCard), calendar data (vCal), and documents. Affects devices with Bluetooth left in discoverable or connectable mode.

How the attack works
  • Attacker uses a directional Bluetooth antenna (range up to 100m) to scan for discoverable devices
  • Sends unauthorised OBEX GET requests to pull files via the Object Push service
  • Vulnerable devices respond automatically before the user sees any prompt
  • Attack takes seconds — entire contact book extracted before victim notices
Warning signs
  • Bluetooth connection attempts from unknown devices in pairing list
  • Brief battery/radio activity when phone is idle in a public place
  • Bluetooth "paired devices" list shows an unfamiliar entry
Defence
  • Turn Bluetooth OFF when not actively using it — this is the single most effective defence
  • Never leave Bluetooth in "Discoverable" mode in public spaces
  • Keep OS updated — all modern iOS and Android versions have patched OBEX vulnerabilities
  • Review Bluetooth paired devices regularly, remove anything unfamiliar
Attack variants
  • BlueJacking — unsolicited message injection (annoying but lower risk)
  • BlueBugging — full device command access via AT command injection
  • KNOB Attack (CVE-2019-9506) — key negotiation downgrade to brute-forceable 1-byte entropy
Bluetooth MITM & Relay Attacks (Multi-Layer)
Layer: L2CAP / RFCOMM · Extended range via relay nodes
▶

Multi-layer Bluetooth relay attacks use a chain of compromised or attacker-controlled devices to extend an attack far beyond the physical Bluetooth range. This is especially used against keyless entry systems, Bluetooth payment terminals, and car unlocking — but the same technique applies to phone-to-device Bluetooth bridges.

How multi-layer relay works
  • Layer 1 (proximity node): A small device placed near your phone picks up your Bluetooth signal
  • Layer 2 (relay): That signal is forwarded over Wi-Fi or cellular to a second device near the target system
  • Layer 3 (exploit node): The second device replays your Bluetooth identity to impersonate you — unlocking a car, approving a payment, or pairing with a trusted device
  • The phone reports a "successful" connection — you never know the relay happened
Warning signs
  • Car unlocked or device connected when you weren't near it
  • Bluetooth pairing confirmations for devices you don't recognise
  • Payment or access event logs showing activity you didn't initiate
  • Unfamiliar Bluetooth device names that appear periodically in public areas
Defence
  • Use Ultra-Wideband (UWB) proximity verification where available (iPhone 11+, Samsung S21+)
  • Enable "require PIN" for all Bluetooth pairings
  • Keep Bluetooth off at rest — relay attacks require your device to be broadcasting
  • For cars: use a Faraday pouch for key fobs; for phone BT, same rule: off when not in use
BLE (Bluetooth Low Energy) Tracking & Fingerprinting
Layer: BLE advertising packets · Passive, no pairing needed
▶

Even without a direct connection, your phone's BLE advertising beacons can be used to track your physical movements across a space. Retailers, venues, and surveillance operators deploy fixed BLE scanners that log your device's MAC address as you walk past — building a movement profile over time.

How it works
  • Your phone constantly broadcasts BLE advertising packets when Bluetooth is enabled
  • These packets historically contained a stable MAC address — uniquely fingerprinting your device
  • A network of cheap BLE scanners (£5 each) can map your path through a building or city
  • Modern Android/iOS randomise MAC addresses — but timing and packet structure can still fingerprint devices
Defence
  • Ensure your device uses BLE MAC randomisation (Android 8+, iOS 14+ do this by default)
  • Turn Bluetooth off in high-security environments (protests, sensitive meetings, border crossings)
  • Settings → Bluetooth → check "Randomise MAC address" is enabled per network if your device shows this option
Category 03

Data Traffic Interception & Injection

Attacks that sit between your phone and the internet — reading, modifying, or rerouting your traffic in real time.

🕸️
Network-Layer Attack Vectors
Targets: credentials, session tokens, DNS, app traffic
CRITICAL
Rogue VPN Apps (Traffic Harvesting)
Access: VPN permission · Full traffic visibility
▶

Free VPN apps are the most dangerous category in app stores. When you grant VPN permission, the app can see 100% of your unencrypted and DNS traffic. Hundreds of free VPN apps have been documented logging and selling user traffic, injecting ads, or acting as full surveillance tools.

How they work
  • Android VPN permission creates a virtual tunnel — all traffic goes through the app before the internet
  • SSL/TLS traffic from HTTPS sites is still encrypted end-to-end but DNS queries reveal every site you visit
  • App can log: all URLs visited, all unencrypted HTTP content, all DNS lookups, app-level traffic patterns
  • Some inject a root certificate to perform a full TLS MITM — breaking HTTPS encryption entirely
Warning signs
  • VPN key icon always visible in status bar even when you didn't activate it
  • App installed itself as a VPN without you requesting it
  • An app that isn't a VPN has requested VPN permission
  • Unexpected certificate warnings from sites that normally load cleanly
Defence
  • Only use VPNs from verified providers with public no-log audits (Mullvad, ProtonVPN)
  • Settings → VPN → check which app is connected; revoke VPN permission from anything suspicious
  • Settings → Security → Trusted Credentials → User tab — delete any certificate you didn't install
  • Never install a VPN app from an ad, SMS link, or non-official store

Evil Twin & ARP Spoofing (Wi-Fi Layer)
Layer: 802.11 / ARP · No app needed — network-level attack
▶

An attacker sets up a Wi-Fi hotspot with the same name (SSID) as a legitimate network — your phone automatically connects to whichever has stronger signal. All your traffic then flows through the attacker's device. ARP spoofing does the same on an existing network by poisoning the network's routing table.

Attack layers
  • Layer 1 (Evil Twin): Clone a known SSID (e.g. "Starbucks Free WiFi"), broadcast stronger signal, capture all connections
  • Layer 2 (ARP Poison): Send fake ARP replies claiming the attacker's MAC is the router — all devices route through attacker
  • Layer 3 (SSL Strip): Downgrade HTTPS connections to HTTP for sites that don't enforce HSTS, capturing plaintext credentials
  • Layer 4 (DNS hijack): Redirect DNS queries to attacker-controlled server, sending users to phishing clones
Defence
  • Use a trusted VPN on all public Wi-Fi — this encrypts traffic before it reaches the router
  • Disable "Auto-join" for public Wi-Fi networks you don't control
  • Check URL bar carefully — SSL stripping turns https:// to http://
  • Enable DNS-over-HTTPS in your browser (Chrome/Firefox settings) to prevent DNS hijack
  • On iOS: Settings → Wi-Fi → (network name) → turn off "Auto-Join" for saved public networks
SS7 Protocol Exploitation (Carrier Level)
Layer: Telecom signalling network · Carrier-level access required
▶

SS7 (Signalling System No. 7) is the 1970s protocol that connects all the world's phone networks. It has no authentication. Anyone with access to an SS7 node (a telecom company, state actor, or purchased dark web access) can intercept any SMS message or phone call on earth — including your 2FA codes.

What attackers can do via SS7
  • Intercept all SMS messages to your number — including bank OTPs and 2FA codes
  • Forward your calls to another number without you knowing
  • Track your rough location via cell tower triangulation globally
  • No app required, no physical access — purely a signalling network exploit
Defence
  • Stop using SMS for 2FA — switch to an authenticator app (Authy, Google Authenticator, or hardware key)
  • Use end-to-end encrypted messaging (Signal) instead of SMS for sensitive communications
  • You cannot prevent SS7 attacks at the device level — the fix is not using SMS as a security channel
